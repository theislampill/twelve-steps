# The Twelve Steps and the prescribed answer

*AA, divine prescription and the Way of the Messengers.*

AA tells the alcoholic to seek God’s help, then supplies the course to follow:
the Twelve Steps. The Big Book leads from surrender through inventory, admission
and amends to continuing prayer and practice. The 12&12 explains reliance on AA
as a beginning of surrender to Providence, and connects practising the Steps with
conformity to God’s will.[^claims][^tt-role] The programme therefore makes a
religious claim for its course. Its authority to make that claim is the concern
of this essay.

The objection is that AA’s recovery experience does not establish its account of
how surrender to God and spiritual restoration are realised through this course.
The programme recommends a spiritual remedy without establishing the warrant for
the religious claims it attaches to that remedy. Calling the Steps suggestions,
leaving the conception of God individual and protecting liberty of creed do not
remove this problem. Freedom about belief can make the religious answer appear
undecided, although a course for seeking and following God has already been offered.

The question is whether God has already given clear, binding and sufficient
religious guidance for this harm. This essay answers yes: the Way of the Messengers,
received through the Qur’an and the teaching of Muhammad ﷺ. On those grounds,
religious guidance must govern the remedy. A human course cannot gain authority to
add terms of spiritual restoration merely because its authors recovered. The
criticism is openly Islamic, with points of contact in AA’s own appeal to God’s
will. Its religious premises are stated and defended in their own right, rather
than inferred from a weakness in AA.

The argument concerns the programme’s religious authority. It does not say that a
person cannot learn revelation elsewhere, stop drinking or receive God’s help.
An ungrounded religious course fails to deliver the prescribed answer; that is
different from saying that no benefit can occur while someone follows it.

## 1. AA makes a religious claim for its course

AA’s course reaches beyond practical advice about avoiding alcohol. In the Big
Book, finding a Power that will solve the problem is the principal object of
*We Agnostics*. Differences in conceptions of that Power are left to individuals,
while the writers’ changed lives commend their approach. Their experience,
reasoning and spiritual interpretation will be called **AA’s case**.[^basis]
That case identifies a course for the reader, not merely an outcome the writers
remember.

The 12&12 gives that course a specific religious meaning. Step Three, p. 35,
interprets entrusting alcohol matters to AA’s guidance as a beginning of surrender
to Providence. Pages 39–40 extend the course to the person’s wider troubles and
describe continuing action on the remaining Steps as necessary for the desired
life. Page 40 connects conformity to their principles with conformity to God’s
will, retaining the qualification “so, we trust”.[^tt-role] This is a fallible
religious judgement, but its fallibility does not make it merely advice about
meeting times or transport. It identifies how surrender to God is to be practised.

The Big Book also assigns spiritual significance to progress through the course.
Pages 72–75 connect inventory and disclosure with a new relationship with the
Creator. They explain repeated drinking after incomplete disclosure as a failure
to finish the necessary personal work. After disclosure, the reader reviews the
first five proposals against the book before proceeding to readiness, prayer for
removal of defects and amends.[^order] The course supplies both practices and an
interpretation of spiritual progress through them.

The objection therefore concerns the religious work done by the programme, not
the existence of twelve items. **Legislative effort** names its selection of a
spiritual course and its account of surrender, restoration and divine help.
The phrase does not allege that AA claims prophetic authorship. The relevant
question is what authorises the religious account it actually recommends.

## 2. Suggestions do not remove that claim

The language of openness can conceal the distinction between leaving belief free
and leaving a religious remedy unspecified. The Steps are suggestions; God is
approached “as we understood Him”; AA protects liberty of creed. Its pamphlets
distinguish a broadly spiritual programme from an organised faith. *The “God” Word*
states: “A.A. is not a religious organization.”[^language] These expressions
describe real features of the invitation. They do not establish that the course
makes no religious claim requiring grounds.

Ibn al-Qayyim’s analysis of a **shubha**—a specious argument in which falsehood
resembles truth—provides a way to examine that inference. He compares attractive
wording to the silver coating on a counterfeit coin. He also describes the reverse
error: truth rejected because it is expressed repugnantly. His instruction is to
judge the meaning impartially after removing the effect of its presentation.[^resemblance]
This essay uses the term in that argument sense. It is an Islamic analytical term,
not AA’s description of itself or a name for every objection to Islam.

State the programme without the reassuring expressions: **AA recommends an
experiential spiritual course, leaves the member’s conception of God individual,
and relates practising its principles to following God’s will.** This is a
paraphrase of the passages already examined. It preserves the fact that the course
is recommended and that its religious interpretation is fallible.

Now restore the expressions: **these are our suggested Steps; approach God as you
understand Him; no common creed is imposed; the programme is spiritual and AA is
not a religious organisation.** The invitation remains to the same course. The
reassuring formulation has neither removed its religious content nor supplied
grounds for its account of God’s will. “Spiritual, not religious” is shorthand
for the distinction the pamphlets make, not a verbatim formula taken from these
passages.[^language]

The false inference is that **freedom of creed removes the need to justify the
religious direction being offered**. A fellowship can decline to impose a creed
while still telling people how surrender to God is realised. Its refusal to settle
a common creed does not leave its proposed remedy empty. In this precise sense,
declining to prescribe a creed coexists with prescribing a spiritual course.
The two formulations can sound different while carrying that same arrangement.

The criticism must then test the course itself. Pleasant wording is no evidence
of falsehood, and stripping it away proves no theological conclusion. The question
exposed by the comparison is whether God’s given instruction authorises the course’s
religious claims. Openness about a member’s beliefs cannot answer that question.

## 3. The Way of the Messengers governs the remedy

Allah’s instruction supplies the religious answer because He knows His creation
and has disclosed how it should worship and obey Him. The Qur’an connects His
knowledge with His creation, directs believers to refer disputes to Allah and the
Messenger, and connects love of Allah with following the Messenger.[^contrast]
Qur’an 5:3 affirms the completion of the religion. Muslim 1718a–b rejects additions
to the religious affair which do not belong to it, and acts not in accordance
with the prophetic command.[^sufficiency] Qur’an 42:21 and 7:33 address unauthorised
religious legislation and speaking about Allah without knowledge.[^authority]
These are the grounds of the judgement here.

The prescribed answer has definite content. Believers are directed to avoid
intoxicants, repent sincerely, repair others’ rights, pray, remember Allah and
seek His help while pursuing benefit. The tradition also addresses habits,
company, despair and renewed effort after a lapse.[^contrast-care] Its sufficiency
concerns religious direction; it does not promise effortless obedience or supply
every clinical decision. The [companion chapter](ISLAMIC_APPLICATION.md#3-what-the-tradition-provides)
develops this work through its sources.

Authorised practical means serve that answer. A teacher can arrange lessons,
someone can keep a written account of their conduct, and a helper can organise
appointments. General instruction can authorise such arrangements without a
separately revealed timetable. Their usefulness does not, however, turn the
teacher’s chosen order into a religious condition of receiving God’s help.
Grounds must reach the status claimed for the arrangement, not merely the
goodness of some acts within it.

This distinction gives the grounding question its force. A course genuinely
authorised by divine instruction escapes this criticism within that authorisation’s
scope. Interpretation and application remain open to correction. A course which
supplies additional religious terms on its own authority does not escape by
describing those terms as useful. Historical authorship is not the test: an act
can have religious grounds its author failed to cite, and a verse cited by a
Muslim author may fail to support the claim attached to it.

## 4. What the programme adds without grounds

AA’s appeal to familiar virtues does not establish the religious status it gives
its complete course. Honesty, prayer and repair have Islamic grounds. Those grounds
authorise the relevant acts under their own conditions. They do not by themselves
establish the 12&12’s identification of reliance on AA with a beginning of surrender
to Providence, or its account of how the course brings conformity to God’s will.
Relying on a helpful group can be reliance on a means Allah provides. That possibility
does not authenticate the programme’s complete account of spiritual restoration.
The disputed claim concerns the religious standing assigned to that account.

The difference becomes concrete in disclosure. The Big Book makes recounting the
life story part of the humbling work it regards as necessary, connects withholding
with renewed drinking, and describes the spiritual experience following completed
disclosure.[^order] Islam gives grounds for repentance, restitution and seeking
advice; those grounds do not establish disclosure on this scale as necessary to
the spiritual progress the book describes. Bukhari 6069 condemns exposing sins
Allah concealed, while Bukhari
1936 records a man disclosing a wrong to obtain the prescribed remedy.[^disclosure]
Together they rule out a simple equation between all disclosure and publicising
sin. They also leave the extent, purpose and recipient of disclosure to be
justified. Confidentiality alone does not establish the need to recount everything.

The objection to Step Five is therefore its prescribed scope and spiritual role,
not a claim that asking someone for help is forbidden. AA does not say that a
sponsor can absolve sin or that disclosure is a condition of Allah’s forgiveness.
Its actual claim is already substantial enough to examine: the course identifies
this disclosure as part of the way to humility, spiritual experience and recovery.
Evidence that confession helped some people can support carefully bounded advice;
it does not establish that account as the religious remedy for the next person.
These passages do not by themselves prove that AA makes disclosure a divinely
prescribed condition. They show the religious account for which its experiential
argument is being offered.

The same distinction applies to self-examination and prayer. Qur’an 59:18 gives
grounds for taking account of one’s deeds; a written inventory can assist that
work.[^accounting] Prayer to Allah and repair of wrongs also have established
grounds. But Step Eleven’s commentary goes further: it links the interwoven
practices to a secure spiritual foundation and assurance concerning God’s kingdom,
conditional on continuing to try to find and do His will.[^tt] Such a religious
assurance needs grounds reaching its content. The practical usefulness of a
worksheet or the general command to pray does not supply them.

**Bidʿah** concerns this authorisation question. In al-Shatibi’s account it is an
invented religious way resembling the ordained way. It is not a synonym for every
new arrangement. He expressly allows later disciplines grounded in general
religious principles. His criticism concerns added religious forms and conditions
which resemble authorised worship without its grounds.[^innovation] The resemblance
can make a practice look authorised; it cannot establish that it is. A shubha can
commend a bidʿah, but proving a misleading argument and classifying a religious
practice are separate tasks.

AA’s recovery case does not warrant accepting its added spiritual necessities and
divine assurances as authoritative religious direction. The judgement rejects the
religious authority assigned to the complete course; it does not declare every act
or religious interpretation within it unauthorised. The objection is to teaching
this course as a further religious way to God, rather than twelve separate rulings
inferred from twelve Step titles. The [source map](APPENDIX.md#1-the-acts-and-the-claims-made-for-them)
distinguishes the acts from the claims attached to them; the [bidʿah discussion](ISLAMIC_APPLICATION.md#5-bidʿah-the-disagreement-and-the-governing-distinction)
also preserves the fivefold Sunni account and its different assessment of some
later arrangements.

This is the displacement of the prescribed answer: AA supplies its own account
of seeking and following God. Permitting revelation to accompany the course as a
member’s belief does not establish revelation as the source determining the
course’s religious terms. The programme’s account of recovery is already supplying
those terms. The conflict lies
in that claim to direct the person spiritually. It requires no later veto of a
known instruction. Nor is it a logical consequence of offering any useful path:
the argument depends on the religious claims just identified and the grounds they
lack.

AA’s offer consequently cannot stand as an authorised completion or replacement
of the Way of the Messengers. Taking useful acts separately does not validate the
religious course from which they were taken. The strongest defence—that AA merely
organises practices a believer can already justify—is answered in
[Objections and replies](OBJECTIONS.md#1-why-isnt-this-simply-a-useful-way-to-practise-existing-teachings).

## 5. What AA’s assurances establish

AA’s assurances show the religious reach of the course and the limits of its
stated grounds. The citations below use printed pages of the official English
editions.[^edition]

| Passage | What the claim says | What needs grounds |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Big Book, p. 58 | “Rarely have we seen a person fail who has thoroughly followed our path.” | The writers’ observation commends the path. It supplies no measured success rate, and thorough participation needs evidence independent of the outcome. |
| Big Book, p. 60 | “That probably no human power could have relieved our alcoholism.” | The probability word and the writers’ own alcoholism limit the claim. Their unsuccessful attempts do not establish the inadequacy of every human means. |
| Big Book, p. 60 | “That God could and would if He were sought.” | God’s capacity and the expectation of His action are distinct claims. The programme must justify what it says seeking Him involves. |
| Big Book, pp. 83–84 | The Promises connect work with changes in outlook, fear, relationships and recognition of divine help. | Conditions of work and gradual fulfilment belong to the promise. They do not themselves establish that the promised results will invariably follow. |

Reported recoveries can be credible without establishing AA’s account of God’s
terms. Human assistance can itself be a means of divine mercy. The error would
be to make an experienced benefit authenticate every spiritual condition or
assurance attached to the course. The same restriction governs this essay’s use
of Islamic accounts of answered prayer.

The failure explanation on p. 58 needs particular care. It attributes failure to
inability or unwillingness to give oneself fully to the programme, usually linked
to incapacity for honesty. If adequate participation is recognised solely by
success, unsuccessful cases cannot test the assurance. Likewise, p. 93’s suggestion
that a religious drinker has failed to apply known precepts cannot establish which
spiritual failing caused that person’s drinking.[^failure] Failure to abstain
establishes non-abstinence; a further diagnosis needs further evidence.

The qualifications must remain intact. The Big Book acknowledges imperfect progress
on p. 60, gradual fulfilment of the Promises, and a daily reprieve on p. 85. The
12&12’s Step Eleven account includes suffering and imperfect attempts to follow
God.[^claims][^tt] These limits prevent caricature. They leave the central question
unchanged: what warrants the programme’s religious account of the path and its
results?

## 6. Recognition of God does not identify His prescription

*We Agnostics* argues towards belief in God without establishing the Twelve Steps
as His religious instruction. It appeals to order in the world, unseen causes,
ordinary trust, inward awareness and spiritual experience.[^agnostics] The relevant
gap is between recognising God and authorising this course in His name.

On p. 53, the writers ask: “God either is, or He isn’t. What was our choice to be?”
For a determinate claim, existence and non-existence exhaust the alternatives;
that disjunction does not decide which is true. A person can also suspend judgement
without proposing a third state of reality. Earlier reasons in the chapter may
support belief, but the urgency of choosing adds no evidence for this particular
account of divine help.

On p. 55, the chapter locates a fundamental idea of God within every person.
Possessing an idea does not by itself establish its object. The stronger reading
is an awareness of God capable of immediate recognition, rather than a proof from
a definition. That has a point of contact with **fitrah**, the original human
disposition described in Islamic sources. The comparison needs neither an
Anselm-style argument nor a dismissal of knowledge which is not reached by a
formal deduction.

Recognition still directs the believer towards what God has disclosed. It does
not supply the forms of worship, conditions of repentance or divine consequences
of a newly arranged course. The [fitrah discussion](ISLAMIC_APPLICATION.md#2-fitrah-and-disclosed-guidance)
develops that distinction while preserving Sunni disagreement over obligations
known before revelation. Awareness of God cannot take the place of knowledge of
His instruction.

## 7. The conclusion

AA’s literature offers religious direction, not merely room for members to bring
religion. It interprets surrender through reliance on its course, attaches spiritual
significance to progress through it, and connects its practices with God’s will
and help. Neither liberty of creed nor reported recovery establishes authority
for those claims.

The Way of the Messengers supplies the religious answer adopted here. Its
instruction must govern the acts, their conditions and the claims made about
Allah’s response. AA’s independently commended religious course cannot complete
that answer or replace it. Naming God as director does not authorise a programme
to supply the religious terms of His direction.

The [companion chapter](ISLAMIC_APPLICATION.md) develops the prescribed work;
[Objections and replies](OBJECTIONS.md) addresses the strongest competing readings.
The [care notice](NOTICE.md#scope-and-care) applies throughout. This essay supplies
no treatment or withdrawal plan.
