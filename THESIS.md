# The Twelve Steps and the prescribed answer

*AA, divine prescription and the Way of the Messengers.*

The Twelve Steps ask a person to seek God’s help for alcoholism while already
offering the way through it. The Big Book sets out belief, surrender, inventory,
admission, amends, prayer and continuing practice as a programme of recovery.[^claims]
God is named as the source of direction; the sequence supplies what that direction
comes to.

The invitation sounds open. The Steps are suggestions, God is approached “as we
understood Him”, and AA distinguishes its spiritual programme from a religious
organisation while protecting liberty of creed.[^language] A reader can hear freedom
about belief as freedom about the answer. Yet the spiritual course is already
supplied. Declining to impose a common creed does not leave the spiritual answer
undecided.

The question is whether God has already given the answer: a prescription that is
clear, non-negotiable and sufficient as the religious way through this harm. If He
has, another spiritual solution has nothing left to complete. It must deliver that
prescription or take its place. AA asks the person to seek His direction after the
programme has supplied the course that direction is to take. The offer can stand as another
solution only by treating the prescribed answer as not already available in that
capacity.

The affirmative answer here is the Way of the Messengers. The Qur’an and the teaching
of Muhammad ﷺ are accepted as revelation from Allah, including the authority and
sufficiency of the religious guidance He has given. Those commitments have their
own grounds; they are not inferred from a gap in AA.

## 1. The answer the programme supplies

AA offers a way through the harm which is not presented on the ground of God’s
given prescription. To stand as another spiritual solution, the offer must treat
the answer as not already available in that prescription as clear, non-negotiable
and sufficient. Its sequence claims a place in supplying the way through. If the
given prescription already settles that answer sufficiently, there is no such place
for another solution to fill.

Calling this a **legislative effort** concerns the work the programme does. It selects
and orders conduct as a spiritual response to the harm, connects that response with
God’s help, and explains how a life is to be brought into conformity with His will.
AA need not call itself a legislature for that role to be examined. On p. 45 the Big
Book makes finding a Power that will solve the problem its main object. Step Three
of the 12&12 connects reliance on AA’s guidance with surrender to Providence and
continuing action on the Steps with conformity to God’s will.[^basis][^tt-role]
These are grounds for applying the description to the published spiritual remedy.
The recovery testimony, reasoning about God and spiritual interpretation offered in
these publications will be called **AA’s case**. The question is what that case
makes the programme’s answer rest on.

Order alone would not suffice. A list arranging transport, meals or appointments
also tells someone what to do next. It does not thereby identify the religious answer
to their condition. The force of the criticism lies in AA’s combination of an ordered
path with an account of spiritual restoration, divine will and the terms of help.
The programme’s [language of openness](THESIS.md#how-the-wording-makes-the-answer-look-open)
needs to be examined alongside that role.

The assumption need not be a belief that God has never issued any binding guidance.
The writers may acknowledge religious precepts, and a member may know and obey them.
The structural claim concerns the offer itself: it stands as the way through where
the prescription would already be the answer. That is the condition the criticism
starts from, not an objection answered by saying that AA never claimed to be a
received legislation.

## 2. What happens to the prescribed answer

Within the offer, God’s prescription is unavailable **as the non-negotiable and
sufficient answer** to the harm. Its content may be known, but the programme has
already supplied the spiritual course it invites the person to follow. God’s given
answer does not wait for a further spiritual sequence to complete it. Recognising it in that capacity would remove the standing of the
offer as another solution. This is why the conflict exists before any later refusal
of an instruction.

This is a real loss of authority even when the instruction’s words remain available.
Receiving a teaching as what God has prescribed differs from selecting it because
it expresses a spiritual programme already commended on other grounds. In the latter
relation, the programme supplies the prior account of the remedy. The teaching enters
as agreement, illustration or personal explanation. Recognition of its content has
not given it the authority to settle the remedy.

The subversion is this displacement of the answer, not an accusation of a hidden
intention. A programme can welcome religious language while retaining the spiritual
solution it has supplied. Indeed, invoking God as the One who directs can introduce
the displacement when conformity to Him is then explained through the programme’s
own sequence. Whether AA does that is assessed from its offered path, not from a
search for a sentence denying God’s power to legislate.

Nor does the criticism demand complete theology from a newcomer before any help can
begin. A person can start with little knowledge. The question is what supplies the
answer they are being led towards. Beginning with incomplete understanding does not
make an independently selected spiritual path the measure of what God has prescribed.

## 3. What grounds the spiritual course

The grounding question tests the assumption just stated. If the offered religious
answer is genuinely grounded in a clear prescription God has given, this criticism
does not arise: the effort delivers that answer rather than supplying another one
to complete it. If it is not, yet stands as the spiritual solution, it occupies the
place already belonging to the prescription. The adverse branch is then present
in the offer itself. A further prohibition of outside guidance is not required.

Grounding is more than resemblance. Two courses may both include honesty, prayer and
repair, yet give different reasons for their authority and different accounts of their
conditions. Agreement with some revealed teachings does not show that the complete
religious answer was derived from them. Nor does a successful outcome retrospectively
make its authors’ arrangement God’s non-negotiable prescription.

Grounding need not mean that revelation supplies a modern timetable or the wording of
every worksheet. Given instruction can authorise an end and appropriate means of
serving it. A teacher can arrange those means while identifying their religious grounds,
keeping their status subordinate, and distinguishing instruction from the teacher’s
choices. What must be grounded is the religious authority actually claimed, including
any specified conditions of divine help. A verse attached to a course does not by itself
ground all its contents or promises.

The test therefore has two branches concerning the offered religious solution.
Practical assistance does not supply a third branch in which an ungrounded solution
keeps its standing. It is a different object of assessment. The distinction between
a religious answer and means of acting on it explains why every calendar is not
another legislation; it does not leave the competing spiritual offer intact under
the description of assistance.

Genuine grounding removes this criticism only within its scope. Whether a text is
authentic, what it means, how it applies, and whether an added arrangement serves it
remain questions requiring evidence. The favourable branch grants no infallibility
to an interpreter or to a programme bearing a religious name.

## 4. God’s direction and the programme’s path

The Big Book moves from the writers’ failure and recovery to a path recommended
to the reader. On p. 50, differences in conceptions of God are left
for individuals to settle while the reported transformation supplies the agreed
reason for taking the path. On p. 60, the writers’ description, reasoning and personal
adventures support their conclusions about God and relief.[^basis][^claims] The
programme is identified through AA’s case, rather than through an established
prescription received from God.

The 12&12 explains reliance on AA’s guidance concerning alcohol as a beginning of
surrender to Providence. It connects continuing action on the Steps with the desired
life, and their principles with God’s will, saying “so, we trust”.[^tt-role] These
are fallible claims, but their subject is substantive: the programme supplies the
spiritual course through which surrender is to be carried out.

### How the wording makes the answer look open

Ibn al-Qayyim explains how a *shubha*, a false argument resembling truth, can gain
acceptance through its expression. He compares fine wording to the silver coating
on a counterfeit coin: the words attract approval while the meaning beneath them
escapes examination. The same proposition can be accepted in one formulation and
rejected in another. His practical instruction is to strip away the wording and
judge the meaning impartially; he also recognises that ugly wording can cause truth
to be rejected.[^resemblance] The comparison must therefore expose a proposition,
rather than use pleasant language as evidence of falsehood.

Consider the programme’s own expressions: the Steps are suggestions; God is approached
“as we understood Him”; members retain liberty of creed. “Spiritual, not religious”
is shorthand for related statements in the pamphlets: the sponsorship guide says
the whole programme may be called spiritual in a broad sense, while *The “God” Word*
says, “A.A. is not a religious organization.”[^language] Together, these expressions
can sound like a refusal to determine the answer. Yet the latter pamphlet’s introduction
defends liberty of creed and then urges readers not to let someone else’s
religious beliefs prevent them from finding the solution available through AA.
Freedom concerning belief and an already offered solution appear together in the
same introduction.

To test the impression of an answer left open, state the offer without its reassuring
descriptions. The following two formulations are this essay’s paraphrases, not
quotations from AA. Their account of what grounds the remedy comes from the sources
just examined; stripping away attractive words could not by itself establish it.

Stated plainly: **the sequence is offered as the spiritual way through this harm;
the conception of God is left individual; a received prescription is not what settles
the programme’s remedy.** The writers’ experience and spiritual interpretation
identify the course. A person is invited to follow it, rather than compelled to
profess one creed. None of this restatement requires the claim that AA declares
itself infallible, that all conceptions are true, or that God never gave instruction.

Put the reassuring language back: **here is our suggested spiritual programme,
drawn from our experience; approach God as you understand Him; AA is not a religious
organisation, and no common creed is imposed.** The sequence remains the proposed
way through. Its grounds remain the experience and interpretation that commended
it. The individual’s conception is still left open. No received prescription has
become what establishes the remedy by changing the description. The substantive
proposition is the same in both formulations.

The resemblance lies in the move from freedom about belief to apparent openness
about the answer. A fellowship can truly refrain from demanding a common creed.
That does not mean the place of the spiritual answer is empty. Here the sequence
has already occupied it. The invitation to understand God individually can make
that occupation sound like leaving the question to the person. What the person
brings is their understanding of God within an offer whose spiritual course is
already supplied.

“Suggestions” presents the offer without coercion; it is still an offer of this
way through. The distinction between spiritual and religious can make the programme
sound as though it has stepped outside the question of religious prescription;
its teaching about surrender and God’s will is still there. AA’s use of *religious*
for an organisation or established faith is not itself a contradiction. The question
concerns what its spiritual teaching supplies. Liberty of creed
allows differing beliefs to accompany the course; it has not made a given prescription
the answer in place of that course. These expressions participate in the presentation
of the solution. They are not merely later defences attached to it.

This is the force of saying that **the decline is the legislation**: the creed is
left individual while the programme supplies the spiritual course. The refusal to
impose a creed is part of how an already determined way through is offered. A received
prescription can then appear as a participant’s religious understanding, while
the programme stands as the way through the harm. The language of non-imposition
makes that arrangement resemble a refusal to determine the answer. The decision
about the answer has already been made through the offer.

The question hidden by that resemblance is whether the solution was **already given,
already clear, already non-negotiable and already sufficient**. On the religious
grounds adopted here, it was. That prescription leaves no unfinished spiritual
answer for the programme to supply. The offer can retain its standing as another
solution only by treating the answer as not already available in that capacity.
Its ordinary language of openness expresses that assumption without announcing a
denial of divine legislation.

God is named as the One who directs, and the sequence is what the direction comes
to. The offer asks the person to seek help from God after taking the place of the
help being sought. If the prescription is available as the sufficient answer, the
offer has nothing to be as another solution. If the offer stands, the direction it
names is not available as that answer. Its self-defeat lies in the condition of
its appeal, rather than in a later refusal. This is an assessment of its use of
divine authority, not a claim that AA expressly cites the Islamic prescription.

AA therefore has no remainder standing as another spiritual solution in front of
the Way of the Messengers. Neither adopting a softer description nor ceasing to
call it an authority leaves that offer intact. The separate question of taking
part in an activity is not the verdict on the offer examined here.

## 5. Why the offer does not deliver the prescribed answer

An offer that supplies another spiritual answer does not deliver the prescription
it displaces. Calling God its director or reporting benefits cannot change that
relation. The sufficient prescription does not become incomplete because a person
needs help acting on it, and another spiritual solution does not become its delivery
because some prescribed acts appear within it.

The claim concerns the offer’s religious authority. People may still learn the
prescription elsewhere, stop drinking or receive God’s assistance. None of those
possibilities gives the programme a remaining place as another spiritual answer.

## 6. The Way of the Messengers

Allah knows His creation and has given guidance for it. The Qur’an connects His knowledge with His creation, directs believers to refer
disputes to Allah and the Messenger, and connects love of Allah with following the
Messenger.[^contrast] People have not been left to invent the religious answer to these
harms. Its authority comes from that disclosure.

Sufficiency belongs to this contrast. Qur’an 5:3 affirms the completion of the religion.
The reports in Muslim 1718a–b reject additions introduced into the religious affair
that do not belong to it, and acts not in accordance with the prophetic command.[^sufficiency] On these
accepted grounds, the Way of the Messengers is non-negotiable and sufficient as the
religious answer. It does not leave an unfinished spiritual remedy for another
programme to complete. Its aversion to an innovation of that kind is the counterpart
of the innovation’s displacement of it.

This conclusion uses authority and sufficiency together. Two practical means can
serve the same end; the mere word *sufficient* does not prohibit alternatives. The
religious premise here is stronger: Allah’s given instruction settles the religious
response, and an unauthorised spiritual answer cannot add to or take its place. AA’s
offered path falls under this criticism because of the religious work identified in
sections 1 and 4, not merely because humans arranged twelve items. In that sense,
the programme is the innovation at issue.

The prescription has content. The Qur’an directs believers to avoid intoxicants,
describes their interference with remembrance, prayer and relationships, calls for
sincere repentance, and forbids despair of Allah’s mercy.[^contrast-care] Repentance
requires leaving the wrong, regretting it, resolving not to return, and discharging
another person’s right where one is involved. Prayer, remembrance, good company and
pursuing benefit while seeking Allah’s help belong to the work of return. These are
elements of the given answer, not additions awaiting a recovery sequence to make
them a spiritual solution. Their sources and practical content are developed in the
[following chapter](ISLAMIC_APPLICATION.md#3-what-the-tradition-provides).

Sufficiency of the religious answer does not mean that hearing a prohibition makes
obedience effortless, that this author’s interpretation is infallible, or that a
particular clinical schedule and outcome have been revealed. The instruction itself
directs action and permits useful means; needing those means does not make the religion
incomplete. Nor does their usefulness establish another spiritual path’s authority.

The fuller chapter carries this same affirmative contrast. Its fitrah discussion
connects recognition of God with disclosed instruction; its practical material sets
out the work that instruction supplies. Its account of Sunni disagreement over bidʿah
and its Millati Islami case test proposed arrangements under the same standard,
including Muslim proposals. The contrast is not derived from a gap in AA, and its
application is not a claim that AA members already accept the Islamic premises.

## 7. What AA says God will do

AA’s assurances tell the reader what God will do in relation to the path. They
attach an account of divine help to the spiritual course already offered. The citations use official English editions and printed page
numbers.[^edition]

### The path and its order

*We Agnostics*, pp. 45–47, identifies lack of power as the writers’ difficulty and
offers a way to find a Higher Power. Willingness and an initially inadequate conception
of God permit a beginning, with further growth expected. On p. 50, differences of
conception are left for individuals to settle while the reported transformation
supplies an agreed reason to take the path.[^basis] This is a substantive account
of the problem and its spiritual remedy, formed through AA’s case.

The sequence does more than number independent tips. After the decision discussed
on p. 63 comes inventory; after inventory comes admission. On pp. 75–76 the reader
checks the first five proposals against the book before proceeding to readiness,
asking for removal of shortcomings, and amends.[^order] The book gives a progression
and standards for examining one’s progress through it. It also invokes a scriptural
maxim about faith and works on p. 76. Such an appeal can support the need for action
without establishing this complete spiritual course from a received prescription.

The 12&12 makes the relation to God especially plain. Step Three, p. 35, treats
entrusting alcohol matters to AA’s guidance as a beginning of surrender to Providence.
Pages 39–40 extend the account to wider troubles and require continuing action on
the remaining Steps for the desired life. Successful practice of them is connected
with persistent trial of Step Three; conformity to their principles is connected
with conformity to God’s will.[^tt-role] These passages establish why the criticism
addresses an offered spiritual solution. The connection is an interpretation of
divine direction made through the programme, not a derivation of the programme from
an authenticated divine prescription.

### The assurances and what they attach to the path

| Passage | Claim and qualification | Its place in the offer |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Big Book, p. 58 | “Rarely have we seen a person fail who has thoroughly followed our path.” | Observed recovery commends the path to the reader; thorough following is a condition. This is not a measured success rate. |
| Big Book, p. 60 | “That probably no human power could have relieved our alcoholism.” | The qualified counterfactual explains the writers’ turn towards divine assistance. Its scope is their alcoholism, not every conceivable person and resource. |
| Big Book, p. 60 | “That God could and would if He were sought.” | God’s action is part of the answer offered, and seeking Him becomes the practical question the ensuing programme addresses. |
| Big Book, pp. 83–84 | The Promises describe changes in outlook, fear, relationships and recognition of divine help. They close: “They will always materialize if we work for them.” | The work of the path is connected with expected spiritual results. The assurance occurs in the amends discussion and permits gradual fulfilment. |

The Big Book acknowledges imperfect progress on p. 60 and a daily reprieve rather than
a permanent cure on p. 85.[^claims] Step Two of the 12&12, p. 33, gives assurance of
restoration through a right relationship with God, rather than promising relief
from one request.[^failure] These qualifications belong to the claims. They do not
reduce the book to a description of whatever happened to its authors: a spiritual
course is being recommended to the next person.

Grant the reported recoveries. Credible testimony can support practical expectations
and, within a wider religious account, recognition of divine mercy. Human help and
divine help need not compete. The p. 60 claim joins what God could do with what He
would do; it does not explicitly deduce the latter from the former. Earlier failures
can support a qualified judgement about the resources tried, while the broader
counterfactual still depends on what *human power* means and what alternatives the
judgement covers.

The structural criticism survives that grant. Evidence that a path helped does not
establish it as the non-negotiable prescription, or disclose a religious deficiency
which that path must fill. The assurances make further claims about what happens
within that course. Assessing them does not replace the question of its religious
authority.

The conditions of the assurances nevertheless deserve direct examination. After the
p. 58 sentence, non-recovery is attributed to inability or unwillingness to give
oneself fully to the programme, usually linked to incapacity for honesty. The text
disclaims fault for people described as constitutionally incapable. If thorough
participation is recognised solely from success, however, the assurance is protected
by definition; evidence of participation must do more than reclassify disappointing
outcomes. Allowing the Promises an unspecified timetable likewise does not establish
exceptionless fulfilment. These are limits on an inference, not diagnoses of members.

Step Eleven of the 12&12 describes security in God’s kingdom while one keeps trying,
however imperfectly, to find and do the Creator’s will, and confidence about the
hereafter. The account includes grief and suffering, not merely worldly comfort.[^tt]
A cumulative religious defence might draw on God’s goodness, moral change and a
tradition’s teaching. Recovery narratives alone do not establish that wider assurance.
Here its significance is that the offered path carries an account of divine help
and spiritual life extending beyond abstinence.

### Existing duties within the programme

Big Book pp. 93–94 recognises that a religious newcomer may know more religion than
the helper. It interprets continuing drinking as failure to apply known precepts,
then directs the helper to outline the programme of action.[^failure] Existing
knowledge is acknowledged, while the programme supplies the practical spiritual
course. Page 94 also appeals to general principles common to most denominations.
That appeal can support shared practices without establishing the religious authority
of the programme’s complete account of the remedy, including its interpretation of
surrender and divine help. The passage does not derive that account from the
newcomer’s prescription.

Its diagnostic inference also needs care. If non-application means only failure to
abstain where abstinence is required, it restates the conduct. If it diagnoses other
spiritual failings as the cause of drinking, it needs the premise that adequate
practice brings the relevant relief. The 12&12’s account on p. 32 of the writers’
failures in humility, amends, service and prayer may describe them accurately without
establishing the same diagnosis for every newcomer.

On p. 74, a denomination’s confession requirement affects the choice of recipient.
The sentence continuing onto p. 75 also expects a confidant who understands and
approves the undertaking and will not alter the plan. Afterwards, progress is checked
against the book’s first five proposals.[^practice] The accommodation takes place
inside an already supplied path. It does not ground that path in the denomination’s
instruction. The continuation concerns this disclosure encounter; the structural
argument has no need to inflate it into a general rule against revelation.

Page 87 recognises required devotions and consultation with religious teachers.
Step Eleven encourages renewed religious practice and warns against mistaking
wishful thinking for God’s direction. Page 164 anticipates further divine
disclosure.[^religion][^discernment] Those provisions matter to how the programme
is used. They do not make a given, sufficient prescription the answer in place of
the spiritual course already offered.

The pamphlets document breadth of participation. *Questions and Answers on Sponsorship*
rejects imposed religious views and recognises sober members without belief in a
personal Higher Power. *The “God” Word* includes agnostic and atheist accounts and
defends liberty to practise one’s creed, principles or therapy against individual
and collective pressure. *Many Paths to Spirituality* includes religious and
non-religious accounts.[^pamphlets] These permissions have their real scope. The
[earlier comparison](THESIS.md#how-the-wording-makes-the-answer-look-open) examines how that
freedom can make the offered answer appear unsettled while leaving the course in place.

### We Agnostics and recognition of God

The chapter’s strongest reasoning is cumulative: the intelligibility of the world,
the limits of familiar assumptions, trust, inward awareness and reported spiritual
encounters can make openness to God reasonable. It is not an Anselm-style argument
deriving existence from a definition.[^agnostics]

On p. 53 the writers ask: “God either is, or He isn’t. What was our choice to be?”
For a determinate claim, existence and non-existence exhaust the alternatives; they
do not decide which is true. The chapter has already offered reasons for belief.
The disjunction adds no evidence for either answer, and suspension of judgement is
not a third state between existence and non-existence. A crisis may make a practical
decision urgent, and trying prayer can be reasonable before every theological
question is settled. That does not turn a practical choice into certainty about
its explanation.

The analogies with unseen causes and ordinary trust have a legitimate target:
things need not be directly visible to be known. An inference to a particular
unseen cause still needs evidence for that cause. Calling confidence in reasoning
a kind of faith does not make every religious belief equally supported.

On p. 55 the chapter claims that “deep down in every man, woman, and child, is the
fundamental idea of God.” An invitation to attend to neglected awareness differs
from a universal psychological finding. Merely possessing an idea does not establish
its object’s existence. The stronger reading is awareness of God capable of immediate
recognition; that deserves examination on its own terms, rather than dismissal for
lacking a syllogism.

Grant that awareness too. It does not by itself specify the forms of worship, the
conditions of repentance or the promised consequences of a newly arranged path.
The [fitrah comparison](ISLAMIC_APPLICATION.md#2-fitrah-and-disclosed-guidance) connects
this point to the affirmative contrast: recognition of the Creator directs attention
to what He has disclosed. It does not establish identity between the doctrines or
historical borrowing. A path does not become the prescription by helping someone
recognise God; the disclosed instruction must itself supply the religious answer.

## 8. The standing of the offer

AA’s literature supplies an ordered spiritual way through the harm, relates it to
God’s will and help, and commends it through AA’s case. It does not establish that
offer as the delivery of the given prescription. The Way of the Messengers supplies
the authoritative and sufficient answer; the offer therefore has no standing as
another spiritual solution or a completion of that answer. Religious accommodation
and reported benefit do not change what supplies the remedy within the offer.
