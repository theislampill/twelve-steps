# Notes on grounds and evidence

The acts a course contains, the religious claims made for them and evidence of
their usefulness require separate examination. This appendix makes those
distinctions checkable without issuing a ruling from each Step’s title.

## 1. The acts and the claims made for them

The table groups relevant Steps by the issue they raise. It paraphrases the
published instructions; it is not a substitute for their wording and commentary.[^steps]

| Steps | Act or claim | What the comparison must establish |
| --- | --- | --- |
| One and Two | Acknowledging the alcohol problem and turning towards a greater Power for restoration. | Admitting difficulty and seeking help do not themselves add a devotion. The identity of the Power and the account of restoration require their own grounds. |
| Three and Eleven | Surrender, prayer and seeking knowledge of God’s will; the commentaries connect these with AA’s course. | Authorisation of prayer does not establish the programme’s interpretation of surrender or its assurances concerning spiritual life. |
| Four and Ten | Inventory and continuing examination of conduct. | Self-accounting has religious grounds. Writing an inventory can be a practical means; its format does not become a divinely prescribed condition merely through usefulness. |
| Five | Admission to God, oneself and another person, developed as disclosure of the life story. | The recipient, extent, purpose and spiritual necessity of disclosure need justification. Confidential advice and publicising sin are not interchangeable. |
| Six and Seven | Readiness and asking God to remove defects. | Grounds for repentance and supplication do not independently establish every proposed stage or result of a recovery sequence. |
| Eight and Nine | Listing harms and making amends, with attention to injury. | Restoring rights has religious grounds. Those grounds govern what is owed and how it is repaired; the programme’s list cannot settle every obligation. |
| Twelve | Describing spiritual awakening as a result of the Steps, carrying the message and practising the principles. | The claimed result and the content being taught need assessment. Transmission of a course does not authenticate its religious claims. |

General authorisation can support later arrangements. The relevant question is
whether the grounds cover the religious status actually claimed, including any
added condition or assurance. A practice’s historical origin outside Islam does
not alone decide that question. Equally, its resemblance to an authorised act
does not establish the complete religious account in which it is embedded.

The [main application](THESIS.md#4-what-the-programme-adds-without-grounds) examines
the disputed additions. The [bidʿah discussion](ISLAMIC_APPLICATION.md#5-bidʿah-the-disagreement-and-the-governing-distinction)
explains why restrictive and fivefold classifications cannot be used as automatic
verdict columns. Both require actual assessment of the relevant religious grounds;
they can disagree over which grounds suffice.

## 2. What continued drinking can establish

The Big Book interprets continued drinking in relation to application of spiritual
precepts. A valid inference requires more than observing the outcome. It needs a
warranted claim that specified practices bring specified relief within the time
being assessed.

Let **D** mean adequate application of those practices and **R** the promised
relief, including its time scope. If `D → R` is true, then `¬R → ¬D` follows by
contraposition. Logic does not establish the initial promise. Nor does continued
drinking now disprove a claim concerning relief at a later, unspecified time.

If D means only abstaining, failure to abstain establishes its absence without
diagnosing another spiritual failing. If D combines several practices, the
premise and absence of R establish only that the combined condition was not met.
They do not identify which element was lacking, or prove that every practice
was absent. A particular diagnosis needs further evidence.

Defining adequate participation solely by successful outcomes prevents an
unsuccessful case from testing the assurance. No Boolean calculation can supply
the missing evidence about participation, causation or the religious promise.
The same constraint applies to a Muslim adviser interpreting continuing difficulty.

## 3. What would change the religious assessment?

Grounds authorising the disputed religious role would change the judgement within
their scope. A warranted interpretation must reach the claim actually made:
the relation to surrender, a specified spiritual necessity, or an assurance about
Allah’s response. General praise for honesty cannot by itself settle all three.

Evidence that a passage recommends a revisable practical means without the
religious status attributed to it would also require correcting that attribution.
The essay retains the sources’ suggestions, probability words and limited claims
for precisely this reason. It does not demand proof that every AA meeting rejects
revelation, and an absence of such rejection does not authorise the published course.

Reported benefit can support practical expectations and recognition of mercy.
It does not by itself establish religious authorisation. Conversely, the authority
of the Way of the Messengers rests on affirmative religious grounds, not on a
failure in AA. Those grounds and their application remain open to substantive
argument; the author gains no infallibility by invoking them.
